# PART 1: THE STORY IN NINETY SECONDS

Nearly 12 million small boats in the US. About 95 percent are under 26 feet and most carry no toilet. Federal rules do not require one on any vessel of any length, so a typical three to five hour trip has nowhere to go.

The person who feels that is the boater. The person who can pay to fix it is the site operator, and those are different people with different budgets. That split is the single most important thing the team learned, and everything in the venture follows from it.

**Salt Pod is a marine sanitation company with two product lines.** The platform line places serviced floating restrooms at water access sites and rents them monthly to operators. The onboard line sells a personal waste pack to boaters directly, distributed through the platform network. The platform reaches the payer. The pack reaches the person who feels the problem. Neither line covers both halves alone.

The platform comes first because a consumable business is a distribution business, and distribution is the function this team is least equipped to start cold. Every deployed platform is a point of sale positioned at the exact moment a boater is experiencing the problem the pack solves.

**Where it stands:** no unit built, no site signed, $290,000 raise, breakeven at month 20 on 15 units, and written agency guidance confirming a four to six week authorization route that competitors do not have.

# PART 4: THE DECK, SLIDE BY SLIDE

What is on it, what sits behind it, and what to say if challenged.

### Slide 1: Title
"A marine sanitation company. Two lines, one network." That line is deliberate. It frames the company as two lines from the first second rather than a restroom business with an idea.

### Slide 2: The Problem
11.2 million small boats, zero bathrooms. The structural point in the note is the split: boaters feel it, sites pay for it. Closures, cleanups, claims are the three cost categories sites actually incur.

**Backing it:** 11.8 million registered and documented US boats in 2024, 95 percent under 26 feet.

### Slide 3: Value Proposition
Today versus with Salt Pod. Note the last row, "Buy it. Permit it. Staff it." against "Rent it." That is the procurement bypass in one line.

**If challenged:** the comparison is against what sites do today, which is portable toilets on land plus a long ride back. Not against another floating restroom.

### Slide 4: Secret Sauce
Folds to trailer width, pontoons on panel ends, 18 inches of draft, off grid and connected. Held as trade secret.

**The honest line, and say it plainly if asked:** a prior art search found folding pontoon patents back to the 1950s including one still active, and the team reversed its plan to file on that basis. Volunteering this reads far better than being caught by it.

### Slide 5: The Permit Nobody Else Asked About (new)
Three pathways. Alongside a public pier is a letter of consent at four to six weeks. Non sovereignty water needs no submerged lands process. Anchored on state bottom is a Board lease at twelve to twenty-four months, which is what the team avoids.

**The mechanism, which is the whole point:** anchoring to the bottom creates new preemption, which forces the lease. Securing alongside a pier rides an authorization the pier already holds. Same product, different attachment, seventeen months of difference.

**Backing it:** Rule 18-21.005(1)(c) lists restroom facilities on public piers as a letter of consent activity, and the definition at 18-21.003 expressly includes portable restrooms. Vessel classification was ruled out because section 327.02 expressly excludes floating structures from the definition of vessel.

**Open question, and say it:** whether a private marina pier meets the public access test. A declaratory statement petition under section 120.565 binds the agency on the facts presented, with a 90 day response requirement.

### Slide 6: Business Model
$2,850 per unit per month, $900 lower touch option, $12,100 fully loaded cost, 5.3 month payback, 80 percent contribution margin, 15 units to breakeven, $489,600 ARR at month 24.

**If challenged on the margin:** servicing, permitting, depreciation, and per unit insurance are all carried before the 80 percent. It is contribution after real operating cost, not gross margin on parts.

### Slide 7: Two Lines, One Network
Platform at $2,850 into a $249M market, recurring. Pack at $3.50 with 66 percent margin into a $1.2B category, repeat. Combined $1.45B. Four phase sequence across the bottom.

**The argument:** every platform is a point of sale, and a boater who just used a Salt Pod a mile from the nearest alternative is the most qualified buyer the pack will ever have.

### Slide 8: Go To Market

**Qualified means** the site already spends $3,000 or more per month on sanitation, so the price is a reallocation of committed budget rather than a request for new budget. **Tier 1 means** a confirmed authorization pathway at four to six weeks.

**This slide was wrong until this week.** It carried a Florida only funnel needing a 62 percent touch rate. Do not quote 62 from memory.

**The real insight, and the line to lead with:** the actual competitor is a build that never got permitted. Twelve of fifteen sites had tried, quoted between $150,000 and $1.8 million, and every attempt was blocked by permitting, flood zones, historic boards, or seagrass protection.

# PART 5: THE SEVEN CORRECTIONS

This is the part of the story most worth knowing, because it is what the instructor praised. Each one made the venture look worse in the short term and more credible overall.

1. **The threefold cost error.** Unit cost was quoted at materials only, $3,505 to $6,125. Adding fabrication labour took it to $12,100 to $15,215. Park Maintains payback went from about 5 months to 16 to 27 months. Caught in the team's own Module 14 work and propagated everywhere.

2. **The patent reversal.** The plan was to file a provisional on the hinge mechanism. A real prior art search found folding pontoon patents back to the 1950s, including US10569843B2 still active. The team concluded the mechanism alone is not broadly patentable and cancelled the filing.

3. **Founder claims walked back.** Module 3 originally described Willem as a commercial boat tour operator and Jason as a marine robotics engineer. Both were revised to the more modest and accurate versions, and capability assessments dropped from Strong to Good.

4. **The Clean Vessel Act conceded.** The CVA was being used as a funding tailwind. It funds pumpout infrastructure for vessels that already have holding tanks, and the entire premise is vessels without them. The synopsis now says it is adjacent rather than aligned and that the team does not count it.

5. **The 33 CFR 159 correction.** Documents said boats under 26 feet are not required to carry a toilet. No vessel of any length is required to install one. The corrected version is stronger, and it means there is no length threshold that could be tightened against the venture.

6. **Vessel classification abandoned.** The buyer work concluded that vessel classification was a central product requirement. The regulatory work then proved it is unavailable under Florida law. Rather than soften the regulatory brief, the buyer document was corrected.

7. **The lost buyer notes disclosed.** Original notes and message threads from the fifteen conversations were lost in a device migration. The team stated it rather than letting it be found.

# PART 6: QUESTIONS YOU WILL GET

**"Has anyone actually agreed to pay?"**
No. Fifteen discovery conversations, seven of seven sites above the spend line accepted the price range verbally, and no site has signed anything. Verbal acceptance in discovery is a signal, not a sale. Getting three written price indications is the next step.

**"Can you show me the interview notes?"**
No. The original notes and message threads were lost in a device migration and the record is reconstructed from a summary made at the time. Figures are recalled ranges, nothing is verbatim, and participants are identified by segment and role. What survives is the pattern rather than the precision, since the same findings appeared independently across unconnected conversations in different segments. **Do not oversell this one. Concede it immediately.**

**"Why is a state park going to fund this when Florida has a $759 million maintenance backlog?"**
Because it is not a capital request. It comes out of an existing operating line that already funds portable toilets, pump out service, and emergency cleanups. Ten of fifteen buyers described a discretionary threshold they personally control that sits above the monthly price. That is the procurement bypass, and it is the single largest determinant of sales cycle length.

**"What if a competitor copies you?"**
On product, they can. On pathway, it is harder. The incumbents build heavy independently moored platforms, which forces them onto the twelve to twenty-four month lease route. Matching a four to six week pier secured pathway means redesigning the product, not adding a feature. Speed and first pilot data are the real defence, and the SWOT says so.

**"You have no patent."**
Correct, and deliberately. The prior art search found the folding pontoon mechanic is decades old with at least one active patent. Filing before a freedom to operate review would spend money on a weak claim. Trademark, NDAs, and IP assignment are the Phase 1 protections.

**"Only $290,000? That seems low."**
It is low because the facility lease is deferred, the founder draw is flat at $4,000, the second hire moved from month 4 to month 7, and the fleet is weighted to the serviced tier. Taking the lease would push breakeven from month 20 to 22 and add about $50,000 to peak cash. The number is sized so the month 19 cash trough holds at roughly twice that month's operating expenses.

**"Is the market big enough?"**
The platform category alone is $249M, which is a specialty market and the team says so. That is why there are two lines. The onboard category is $1.2B today and $2.7B by 2030, and combined addressable market is $1.45B. The platform is the distribution network the larger business runs on.

**"What is the biggest risk?"**
Two. Whether the payer feels the urgency the user feels, which is what the buyer work exists to test and is not yet closed. And whether a private marina pier qualifies for the fast authorization pathway, which decides whether the highest spending segment is reachable in weeks or years. A declaratory petition is being filed on the second.

**"What is unpriced?"**
Servicing at $300 per unit per month is modelled from a two visit schedule, not quoted, and it is the largest unvalidated operating input. The 160 hour build has never been tested against a timed build. The 7 percent close rate and the 3 percent attach rate are both modelled.
