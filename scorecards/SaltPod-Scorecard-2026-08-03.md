## 1. Executive Summary

Between July and August the team rebuilt the analytical foundation of this venture, and the work is materially better than what was scored six weeks ago. Market sizing moved from a top-down category figure to a bottom-up build with a screenable qualification criterion and a full sensitivity register. The pro forma now carries servicing, permitting, insurance, and depreciation on every deployed unit — costs that were entirely absent before. Most consequentially, the team discovered that their own unit cost had been understated by roughly three times, corrected it from $3,505–$6,125 (materials only) to $12,100–$15,215 (fully loaded with fabrication labor), and propagated that correction through every downstream document.

**Total Score: 31/80 — down 3 points from July's 34.**

The decline is not a deterioration in the business. It is the arithmetic consequence of replacing optimistic placeholders with real numbers. Two dimensions moved: Cost of Sales (5 → 3) once the funnel math showed the motion requires touching 55–85% of the entire qualified Florida market to place 15 units, and Profitability (5 → 4) once depreciation, permitting, and insurance pushed venture-level breakeven past 24 months. July's higher score rested on inputs the team has since proven wrong. A scorecard that rewarded the earlier, less accurate numbers was measuring the documentation, not the venture.

The defining pattern in this cycle: **every improvement item that could be completed at a desk was completed, and completed well. Every item requiring contact with a person outside the team was not.** Of the six actions recommended in July, one was executed (pricing the unpriced cost categories), one was deliberately and correctly reversed on evidence (the provisional patent), and four remain open — the marine-industry advisor, the real park conversation, the physical build, and the willingness-to-pay survey. The pattern rule that triggered in July triggered again, unaddressed.

The venture's central unknown is unchanged and now stated explicitly by the team itself: the boater feels the pain, the site operator pays the invoice, and nothing in the evidence base establishes that the payer feels urgency.

## 3. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### 1. Founder-Market Fit — M (2) · Confidence: High

**Evidence.** The team carries real capability: Brandon is a chemical engineer with environmental and regulatory literacy; Jason builds rocket engines and has prior entrepreneurial experience; Willem brings ten years of operations and distribution and is now named Founder/CEO in the pro forma at a $4,000/month draw — a genuine commitment signal. The analytical quality across all 16 documents is itself evidence of capability.

**Why it does not move.** The scorecard measures team-market fit, not team quality, and the market-specific gap is unchanged. No one has marine fabrication, naval architecture, marina operations, or Florida B2G procurement experience. This matters concretely: the GTM path runs through park managers, FDEP submerged-lands leasing, marine liability underwriting, and ADA compliance certification — four domains where the team has no network.

The team identified this correctly in Module 18, naming it Priority 2 and noting that "an investor reading our pitch deck will ask who is on the team almost immediately." The proposed fix was to name a target advisor even before the relationship was finalized. The pitch deck instead states: *"Known gap: dedicated marine fabrication and naval architecture experience. Planned for Phase 3."* Deferring the acknowledged gap to the final phase is weaker than the team's own plan, and it leaves the pattern-rule violation open.

**Risk.** Every Gate 1 item in the pro forma — an FDEP determination, a real insurance quote, a first park conversation — sits in the exact domain where the team has no relationships.

### 2. Market Size — M (2) · Confidence: High

**Evidence.** Module 10 is a substantial upgrade in method. Rather than citing a global category figure, it builds from US site counts across six segments, applies a qualification criterion (current sanitation spend ≥ $3,000/month) derived from the buyer persona work, and multiplies by unit density and blended pricing:

- 26,700 US sites → 6,104 qualified → 9,156 units at 1.5/site → **$249M TAM** at $27,180 blended annual revenue per unit
- SAM (Southeast/Gulf, trailerable from Florida): **$80M**
- SOM (Florida, 5-year, 30% penetration of 252 qualified sites): **$3.08M ARR from 113 units**

I re-derived all three figures; the arithmetic is correct throughout, and the assumption register discloses sensitivity honestly (a 1.0 unit-per-site assumption cuts TAM to $166M).

**Why it does not move.** $249M sits in the $100M–$500M band. Notably, the more rigorous bottom-up figure is *lower* than July's top-down $332M–$459M — better method, smaller number, same score band. The team states the cap plainly: "At approximately $249M the qualified platform category is a specialty market rather than a venture-scale one on its own. That is a structural property of the category and execution does not change it."

**The escape route exists and is documented.** Path B — onboard marine sanitation devices — is a category I independently verified at $1.2B (2023) growing to $2.7B (2030). Combining it with the platform business is, per the team's own analysis, "the only path that credibly clears $1B in addressable market." It is explicitly held as an option with a decision point at the end of the Florida pilot. Because it is not the current thesis, it cannot be scored here — but it is the single lever that moves this dimension.

### 3. Customer Urgency — L (3) · Confidence: Medium

**Evidence for.** The end-user pain is well documented and unchanged from July: unprompted boater testimony that women and children drive the decision, trips cut short, the "they always ask if the boat has a bathroom" pattern. Module 7/8/9 adds a genuinely valuable section cataloguing what the problem has already cost sites — E. coli beach closures with camper refunds and ranger overtime, a $2,500 emergency holiday plumbing call, lost eco-tour contracts, recurring biohazard janitorial calls, sportfishing charters lost to a competitor over facility quality. Money already lost implies a budget exists to stop losing it.

**Why it cannot move above 3.** The fifteen buyer profiles are explicitly labelled by the team as "constructed personas grounded in published operating budgets... They are not transcripts of customer interviews." The documented costs above are modeled attributes of fictional sites, not incidents reported by real ones. The Buyer Validation Interview Guide is an excellent instrument — Rule 1 ("ask about the past, not the future") and Rule 2 ("never pitch before question 11") show real methodological discipline, and the scoring threshold is pre-registered at 3 of 15 naming a specific budget line. It has not been fielded.

Module 16 states the problem with unusual candour: *"The boater feels the pain. The invoice goes to a facilities budget. Those are different people with different incentives, and nothing in our work to date establishes that the payer feels urgency."* The team is right, and that sentence is the reason this dimension holds at 3.

**Pattern-rule note.** Exactly 3 for the second consecutive scorecard — still sitting on the failure-predictor boundary, still unresolved by evidence.

### 4. Competition — L (3) · Confidence: High

**Evidence.** The competitive work is the most improved analysis in the set. The reframe is genuinely insightful: asked what they had already tried, 12 of 15 profiles described attempting to build a permanent shoreside facility, and every attempt died — $1.8M for underwater trenching and a five-year runway, $450K for sewer lines through a seawall, blocked by a Historic Preservation Board on sightlines, rejected outright by a water authority near a drinking reservoir. The conclusion follows: *"A mobile, non-permanent, vessel-classified platform is not competing on price against other floating restrooms. It is competing on whether the project can happen at all."*

Section C on the status quo is equally strong and correctly names doing nothing as the true baseline — free, normalized, and beatable only through behaviour change.

**Why it does not move.** The reframe is analytically excellent, but the competitive facts are unchanged. Four verified, entrenched, funded competitors operate in the adjacent category, and the team's own honest read is that PumpOut USA "has no logistics weakness to exploit, they are already local, already funded, and already trusted by the exact counties we want to approach." Compo Closet is separately identified as actively closing the compactness gap. That is "moderate competition with room for differentiation," not "few competitors with significant gaps."

The shallow-draft whitespace (18-inch operating depth, standard pontoon trailer) is real and defensible. It is not yet blue ocean.

### 5. Novelty — L (3) · Confidence: High

**Evidence.** The IP Strategy Brief is the most intellectually honest document in the folder. The team ran a real patent search and reported findings that *weaken* their own position: a direct lineage of folding-pontoon filings from US2588084 (1950s) through US3099977 and US3763511, including the still-active **US10569843B2, "Transportable folding boat structure"** — which I independently confirmed exists (inventor Aaron Kiellerup, application US16/073,147). Their conclusion: "the core hinge-and-pontoon mechanic itself is decades old and partially still under active patent," and therefore "the mechanism alone likely isn't broadly patentable."

They also attempted to verify Marine Boatbuilders' claimed "patent pending" Float Ballast System, could not find it in public records, and correctly recorded it as an open question rather than asserting either way.

**Why it does not move.** Differentiation is in deployment method and cost structure, not in a new-to-the-world innovation. The candidate novel claim — a fixed base platform plus independently hinged, pontoon-ended panels applied to a sanitation platform — is narrow. The category itself is public domain, documented by state marine boards. L(3) is correct: "distinctive approach with clear differentiation, but core use case is familiar."

**Credit where due.** Reversing the July recommendation to file a provisional patent was the right call, made on real evidence. It costs nothing on this dimension and demonstrates exactly the analytical integrity an investor wants to see.

### 6. Resources Required — M (5) · Confidence: High

**Evidence.** This dimension is now honestly costed for the first time, and the correction was severe. Module 12 states it plainly: "These figures supersede all earlier parts-only cost estimates."

| Phase | Materials | Labor | Fully loaded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prototype (160 hrs) | $4,815 | $10,400 | **$15,215** |
| Small batch (120 hrs) | $4,300 | $7,800 | **$12,100** |
| Mass production (90 hrs) | $4,000 | $5,850 | **$9,850** |

Venture-level: a $290,000 raise, two W-2 employees, subcontracted fabrication, peak cumulative cash need of approximately $221,000.

**Why it holds at 5 despite tripling.** The criteria for M(5) are "moderate resources; small dedicated team, seed funding, some custom development." A $290K raise with two employees fits squarely. L(3) would require Series A/B funding and a larger team. The unit cost tripled but the venture remained capital-efficient, and the team made deliberate choices to keep it that way — deferring the facility lease, holding the founder draw flat, moving the Operations Lead hire from month 4 to month 7.

**Note on discipline.** The team explicitly declined to model an indefensible learning curve: "a 60-hour small-batch assumption would imply a 62 percent reduction by the second unit and is not defensible." Choosing the less flattering assumption is the correct instinct.

### 7. Time to Market — L (3) · Confidence: Medium

**Evidence.** No unit has been built. The pro forma models a five-month build with first deployment in month 6, contingent on funding closing in month 1.

Module 15 is a real engineering plan — 23 capabilities with quantified metrics and thresholds, classified atomic vs. composite, and sequenced with defensible reasoning (structure first, shallow-draft second because "it's the actual reason this beats a six-figure competitor"). It is also, read honestly, a long list of things that must be proven: zero leaks over 50 use cycles, no structural failure in sustained 40-knot winds, no functional degradation after 90 days of saltwater exposure, ADA dimensional compliance, ≥85% Starlink uptime over 30 days, ≥90% of test users completing a use cycle unassisted.

**The gating unknown is regulatory, not technical.** Whether the unit is classified as a vessel or a permanent structure determines everything. Module 7/8/9 captures both sides: one profile requires vessel classification specifically to bypass permanent-building bans; another warns that structure classification in a navigable waterway means a five-year approval. The team correctly calls this "a central product and regulatory requirement, not a detail." It remains undetermined.

L(3) — "6–12 months; fairly complex with technical unknowns" — is the right band. Better understood than in July, not faster.

### 8. Cost of Sales — L (3), down from M (5) · Confidence: Medium

**This is a revision on new evidence, not a deterioration in the business.** July scored this M(5) at Low-Medium confidence, explicitly labelled "a structural read of the GTM plan, not observed performance." Module 16 now supplies the actual funnel math, and it points down.

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Average revenue per unit | $27,180/year |
| Expected close rate | 7% (below the 15–20% competent benchmark, since nothing has closed) |
| Units required for $300K target | ~11 |
| Opportunities required | ~157 |
| Qualified Florida sites | 284 per Module 16 (**252** per current Module 10 — see Critic) |
| Market touch rate required | 55% (**85%** against the corrected base) |

Placing eleven units requires meaningful contact with somewhere between half and five-sixths of every qualified site in Florida. The pro forma's own note concedes the 15-unit fleet needs 214 opportunities — "demanding, but inside the addressable base." Each opportunity runs a seven-stage funnel including a physical site visit, a written proposal covering insurance and ADA documentation, and internal approval that may require a second demo for decision-makers.

That is the definition of L(3): "significant; high-touch, relationship-driven sales with sophisticated buyers."

**The genuine counter-argument, and why it does not carry the score.** The procurement-bypass insight is real and valuable: every profiled site has a discretionary operating threshold the monthly rate sits beneath, and a capital process measured in quarters. Renting instead of selling moves the decision into the fast lane, and the team is right that this belongs at the front of the pitch. But that compresses *cycle length*, not *cost per acquisition*. Deck-claimed CAC of $6,500 against $27,180 annual revenue is plausible, though it appears in no other document.

### 9. Profitability — 4, down from 5 · Confidence: High

Average of two sub-scores.

**Profit Margin Potential — XL (5), unchanged.** Self Maintains contribution is $2,246/month on $2,850 revenue — a 79% margin *after* servicing ($300), permitting ($75), depreciation ($144), and per-unit insurance ($85). I verified this arithmetic directly against the assumptions tab. Park Maintains nets $596. Phase 2 Self Maintains gross margins run 59.7%–71.2%. These are genuinely strong, and they are now fully loaded rather than aspirational.

The team also discloses the unflattering half: Park Maintains Year 1 gross margin is negative 44% at the low end of the band, and they state plainly it "should not be the tier we lead a first conversation with."

**Time to Profitability — L (3), down from M (5).** The pro forma carries fleet operating costs that were entirely absent in July; the team's own note says earlier versions "overstated profit on every deployed unit." With those costs in:

- Monthly gross profit turns positive in **month 20** (the notes say month 19; month 19 is −$298.25 and month 20 is +$1,947.75 — see Critic)
- Year 1 cumulative gross profit: **−$171,670**
- Cumulative venture-level profitability falls beyond the 24-month model horizon; the pitch deck itself says the raise "gets us to Year Three, cash flow positive"

That places time-to-profitability in the 18–36 month band, which is L(3), not M(5).

**Average: (5 + 3) / 2 = 4.**

The whole of this one-point decline traces to costs the team correctly added. A model that omits depreciation and permitting scores better and predicts worse.

### 10. Market Timing — L (3) · Confidence: Medium-High

**Verified tailwinds.** The Clean Vessel Act is confirmed active, funded from the Sport Fish Restoration and Boating Trust Fund at roughly $10M annually with a 2024 distribution exceeding $14 million. US registered and documented boats stand at 11.8 million as of 2024, with 95% under 26 feet — approximately 11.2 million, matching the pitch deck's headline figure exactly. The recreational boating market continues to grow.

**Unchanged headwind.** The team's own SWOT states that FDEP's "existing incumbent relationships already favor PumpOut USA," and that agency approval cycles run "months to years, not weeks." Grant funding is subject to political and budget cycles. Regulatory tightening of the under-26-foot exemption remains speculative.

Module 13 Exercise 2 adds a thoughtful resilience argument: if the exemption tightened, paddlers, kayakers and canoeists physically cannot comply on-vessel, making external infrastructure their only legal option. That is a genuine asymmetry worth having ready for an investor question, though it is a hedge against a hypothetical rather than a current tailwind.

Nothing material changed since July. L(3) holds.

## 5. Structural Analysis

**Strengths (scoring 5+):** Resources Required (5) stands alone. The venture is capital-efficient, honestly costed, and buildable on a $290K raise — genuinely rare for hardware.

**Weaknesses (scoring 2 or below):** Founder-Market Fit (2) and Market Size (2), unchanged from July and for unchanged reasons.

**Structural misalignments:**

- **Excellent analysis, absent external contact.** Every desk-executable improvement was completed to a high standard. Every improvement requiring a phone call was not. Four of the six July recommendations — advisor, park conversation, physical build, willingness-to-pay survey — all share the property of requiring someone outside the four founders to respond. This is the venture's defining constraint, and it is behavioural rather than analytical.

- **Strong unit economics resting on an unvalidated payer.** The 79% contribution margin is real arithmetic on a modeled price. No site has agreed to $2,850/month. Module 16's failure signal is well specified — contacts express interest but cannot name a budget source — and the team correctly notes such a result "would indicate that the buyer, not the solution, is the thing to change." That test has not been run.

- **A rigorous financial model and a pitch deck that contradicts it.** Pro Forma v3 explicitly supersedes the $400,000 raise with $290,000; the deck asks for $400,000. Module 16 explicitly retires the 260-opportunity funnel as unachievable; the deck runs it. The pro forma models $34,800 of Year 1 revenue; the deck promises $300,000. An investor reading both will trust neither.

- **Feasibility advantage gated by an unanswered regulatory question.** The strongest competitive insight — that the real alternative is a construction project blocked by permitting — depends entirely on Salt Pod being classified as a vessel rather than a structure. If that classification goes the wrong way, the central differentiator inverts into the central obstacle.

- **Correct diagnosis of the market-size cap, decision deferred.** The team knows Path B (onboard sanitation, independently verified at $1.2B→$2.7B) is the only route to a venture-scale market, and has scheduled the decision after the Florida pilot. That sequencing is defensible operationally but means Market Size cannot move within the current planning horizon.

## 7. Overall Assessment and Recommendation

**Score interpretation.** 31/80 falls in the "Below 40 — Very Poor" band. Two dimensions score at the floor, and nothing scores above 5.

**Read the number correctly.** This scorecard underwrites for institutional, venture-scale returns. On that specific question the answer is unambiguous: no built product, no customer conversation, no signed agreement, a team missing the domain network its GTM requires, and a category ceiling around $249M. It does not clear that bar.

That is a different question from whether this is good work or a viable business. The analytical quality in this data room exceeds what most pre-seed companies produce, and in three places — the fully loaded cost correction, the prior art search that undercut their own IP position, and the explicit statement that payer urgency is unvalidated — the team chose the answer that made them look worse because it was true. That instinct is worth more than three scorecard points.

The honest summary is that Salt Pod is a well-analyzed, capital-efficient, plausibly profitable small business in a specialty category, currently documented to a standard that would embarrass most seed-stage decks, and entirely unvalidated outside the founders' own spreadsheets.

**Go/No-Go: No-Go for venture-scale investment at this stage.** Unchanged from July, for substantially better-evidenced reasons.

**Recommended engagement mode: Structured Support.** Capital exposure is low, the analytical foundation is strong, and the binding constraint — no external contact — is precisely what structured mentorship and an advisor placement address. Internal Origination and Strategic Partnership remain premature until a pilot exists. Asset Transformation does not apply. A hard Pass would be the wrong call on work of this quality.

**Conditions for re-scoring:**

1. Fifteen completed buyer conversations against the Module 9 threshold, with the three verbatim fields recorded.
2. A named advisor with marine-industry or Florida government-contracting experience, in the deck.
3. A written FDEP position on vessel-vs-structure classification.
4. One built and tested Phase 1 unit with results against the Module 15 capability thresholds.
5. Real quotes for insurance and permitting replacing the modeled figures.
6. A documented decision on Path B.

Items 1, 2, 3 and 6 are achievable in 30–60 days at near-zero cash cost and would plausibly carry the total into the mid-40s. All four have been available for the past six weeks.
